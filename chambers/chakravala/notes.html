03 · चक्रवाल · the wheel

Brahmagupta (628) → Jayadeva (11th c.) → Bhāskara II, Bījagaṇita (1150)

The problem

Find whole numbers x, y with x² − N·y² = 1 (a "vargaprakṛti" problem; Europe later named it after Pell). For N = 61 the smallest solution is

$$1\,766\,319\,049^2 - 61\cdot 226\,153\,980^2 = 1,$$

the case Fermat famously posed as a challenge in 1657, about five centuries after Bhāskara II had solved it [selenius1975].

One turn of the wheel

Keep a triple (a, b, k) with a² − N·b² = k. Pick m > 0 so that k divides a + b·m and m² is as close to N as possible. Then

$$a' = \frac{am + Nb}{|k|},\quad b' = \frac{a + bm}{|k|},\quad k' = \frac{m^2 - N}{k}.$$

The invariant a'² − N·b'² = k' holds after every turn. That's Brahmagupta's composition rule (bhāvanā), divided through by k². Choosing m near √N keeps |k| small, and the cycle always returns to k = 1. My tests check the invariant at every step for ten values of N, and that N = 61 lands exactly on Bhāskara's numbers.

while (k !== 1n) {
  const K = |k|;  let m0 = 1n;
  while ((a + b*m0) % K !== 0n) m0++;         // k | a + b·m
  m = the m ≡ m0 (mod K) with |m² − N| smallest;
  [a, b, k] = [(a*m + N*b)/K, (a + b*m)/K, (m*m − N)/k];
}

Why it's remarkable

Selenius (1975) showed the chakravāla is a best-approximation continued-fraction process of minimal length. For N = 61 he counts five or six steps with Bhāskara's shortcuts against about 22 convergents in the Euler–Lagrange approach [selenius1975]. The plain version drawn here takes 13 turns. Each a/b is a rational approximation of √N, and the readout shows the digits locking in.

Reading the picture

Each spoke is one turn. Its distance from the centre is the number of digits in a, and the size of its petal is |k|. The wheel closes, and the bowl rings, when k reaches 1.
