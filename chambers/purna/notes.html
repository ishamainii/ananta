07 · पूर्ण · three ways to hold the infinite

Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad · Jaina sūtras (c. 300 BCE onward) · Bhāskara II, Bījagaṇita (1150)

I · pūrṇa, the whole

पूर्णमदः पूर्णमिदं पूर्णात्पूर्णमुदच्यते । पूर्णस्य पूर्णमादाय पूर्णमेवावशिष्यते ॥

"That is whole, this is whole; from the whole the whole arises; taking the whole from the whole, the whole remains" (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.1.1) [brihad511]. It's a spiritual statement, not a theorem, but it captures exactly what makes infinite sets strange. A set can lose a part the same size as itself and stay the same size, like the even numbers taken out of the whole numbers.

The picture is a Droste spiral. In logarithmic coordinates w = log z, the pattern repeats every time |z| grows by a factor of 3.2, and a twist shifts the angle by a full turn per repeat, so the stacked rings fuse into a single spiral:

$$\tau = \log|z| + \frac{\arg z}{2\pi}\log 3.2 - 0.11\,t,\qquad \text{pattern}(\tau \bmod \log 3.2,\ \arg z)$$

Sliding τ with time is a zoom with no bottom: the whole keeps arising from the whole.

II · saṅkhyāta, asaṅkhyāta, ananta

Jaina mathematics classed every number as enumerable, innumerable or infinite, and recognised several kinds of infinity (in one direction, in two, in area, everywhere, perpetually) [mactutorjaina]. The Anuyogadvāra Sūtra builds its first "innumerable" number by a thought experiment of filling huge containers with mustard seeds, again and again [gupta1992]. In the picture, each ring holds ten times the last until it can no longer be drawn seed by seed.

III · khahara

Bhāskara II calls a quantity with zero as its divisor khahara. Bījagaṇita 2.20 says that adding or subtracting quantities does not change it, and compares it to the infinite, unchanging divine [sathaye2015, colebrooke1817]. The graph shows 1 ÷ x as x slides to 0, then that rule in action. It's not modern analysis: today 1/0 is left undefined, and we'd say instead that 1/x grows without bound as x → 0⁺. But it's a clear statement of how ∞ absorbs finite additions, the property Cantor's cardinal arithmetic made precise (ℵ₀ + n = ℵ₀).
